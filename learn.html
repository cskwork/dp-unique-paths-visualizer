The Dynamic Programming Course

Dynamic programming, one small step at a time

This course is for anyone, including people who have never written a line of code. Dynamic programming has a scary name and a simple heart. You will meet the idea in plain words, watch one tiny example solved by hand right on this page, then climb four problems in order, each with a clear "you have cleared this level when" test. No prior algorithms needed. Bring patience and a willingness to press play.

Start level 1: Climbing Stairs See the full path

The Core Idea

Solve each small piece once, then reuse it

Picture a cook with a notepad. Every time they finish a sauce, they write it down. When a later dish needs that sauce, they read the notepad instead of making it again. Dynamic programming is exactly that habit, applied to problems: break a big question into smaller versions of itself, solve each small version once, write the answer down, and reuse it forever after.

01

Break it down

A big question is usually made of smaller copies of the same question. The answer for 10 stairs leans on the answer for 9 and 8.

02

Write each answer down

Keep a row of boxes, one per small version. Fill the easiest boxes first, then use them to fill the harder ones.

03

Never redo work

Once a box has a number, it is final. Every later box just reads the boxes it needs. That reuse is the whole speed-up.

See It Once

One tiny problem, solved by hand

Words only go so far, so here is the whole trick on four stairs. You can climb 1 or 2 stairs at a time, and you want to know how many different ways there are to reach the top. Watch the boxes fill. This takes one minute, and it is the same move every DP problem in existence makes.

1

Draw one box per stair. Each box will hold a single number: how many ways there are to reach that stair. Right now nobody knows any of them, and that is fine.

stair 1 stair 2 stair 3 stair 4
2

Fill the easy ones with your fingers. Stair 1 can be reached exactly one way: a single small step. Stair 2 can be reached two ways: two small steps, or one double step. No formula, just counting on one hand.

1stair 1 2stair 2 stair 3 stair 4
3

Let one rule fill the next box. Every way to stand on stair 3 arrived from stair 2 (a small step) or from stair 1 (a double step). There is no third door in. So the box for stair 3 is simply the two earlier boxes, added.

1read 2read 3write stair 4

box 3 = box 2 + box 1 = 2 + 1 = 3

4

Repeat, and the answer falls out. The same rule fills stair 4 from stairs 3 and 2. Five ways to climb four stairs, and notice what never happened: no route was ever listed, and no box was ever computed twice. The boxes did all the work.

1stair 1 2read 3read 5write

box 4 = box 3 + box 2 = 3 + 2 = 5

Why Bother

What writing answers down actually buys you

Four stairs is cute. The trick matters at forty. Without the notepad, a computer answers "how many ways up 40 stairs?" by re-asking the same small questions over and over, because every big question re-asks the two below it, and each of those re-asks its own two. With the notepad, every question is asked once, answered once, and read for free ever after.

Without the notepad 331,160,281

little questions asked to count the ways up 40 stairs, almost all of them repeats of one another. The pile roughly doubles with every extra stair.

With the notepad 40

boxes filled, one per stair, each with a single addition. Stair 41 costs one more box, not double the work. That is the entire speed-up, and you just watched it.

The Setup

Four questions that unlock any DP problem

You do not start by writing code. You start by answering these four questions in plain words. Every problem in this course is just these four answers, filled in. One naming note before they appear: in code, the row of boxes you just watched is called dp, and box number i is written dp[i]. Same boxes, shorter name. The first question matters most: if you cannot say what a saved answer means, nothing after it will make sense.

Question 1

What does each saved answer mean?

Finish this sentence out loud before anything else: dp[i] is the ___. The number of ways to reach stair i. The cheapest cost to reach it. The best haul up to house i.

Question 2

What is the choice at each step?

Are you counting every route (add), paying for one (keep the min), or picking items (keep the max)? The choice decides whether you add, min, or max.

Question 3

How does a bigger answer use smaller ones?

This is the recurrence: one short line saying how dp[i] is built from earlier boxes like dp[i-1] and dp[i-2].

Question 4

Which cases can you fill in directly?

The base cases: the first box or two that you know without any formula. Get these right first, then let the recurrence handle the rest.

The Path

Four levels, each adding exactly one idea

Climb in order. The first three problems share the same picture, a single row of boxes filled left to right, and change only one thing each time: first you add, then you keep the cheaper, then you keep the better. The fourth turns that row into a grid. By the end, the grid problem that looked hard on day one reads as one more small step.

1

Climbing Stairs

Easy

New idea: add the two ways in. Count how many ways there are to climb a staircase. To reach a stair, add the ways to reach the two stairs below it. This is the worked example above, grown up, with a play button.

Cleared when you can explain, without looking at the formula, why the ways into a stair are exactly the ways to the stair below plus the ways to the stair two below.

dp[i] = dp[i-1] + dp[i-2] Start level 1
2

Min Cost Climbing Stairs

Easy

New idea: keep the cheaper. Now each stair has a price. The two routes into a stair are still there, but instead of adding them you keep the cheaper total. Add becomes min.

Cleared when you can say in one sentence why the plus turned into a min the moment stairs got prices.

dp[i] = min(dp[i-1]+cost[i-1], dp[i-2]+cost[i-2]) Start level 2
3

House Robber

Medium

New idea: take it or skip it. A row of houses, no two neighbors allowed. At each house, weigh skipping against robbing, and keep the larger haul. Here the choice keeps the max.

Cleared when you can name the two choices at any house, skip it or rob it, and say which earlier box each choice reads.

dp[i] = max(dp[i-1], dp[i-2]+nums[i]) Start level 3
4

Unique Paths

Medium

New idea: one row becomes a grid. The same reasoning, now in two dimensions. A robot counts paths across a grid, and each cell adds the cell above and the cell to its left. The row you mastered grows a second axis.

Cleared when you can point at any cell in the grid, name the two cells it adds, and say in words what its number means.

dp[r][c] = dp[r-1][c] + dp[r][c-1] Start level 4

Common Traps

Three habits that prevent most beginner mistakes

Say what the box means, in words

Most stuck moments trace back to a fuzzy state. Write one plain sentence for dp[i] before you write any formula.

Get the base cases right first

The easy first boxes are where quiet bugs hide. Fill and hand-check them before trusting the recurrence.

Do not shrink memory too early

Get the full row of boxes correct first. Compressing it down to two variables is a separate, later step.

After The Course

The road from here to mastery

The four levels teach one shape: a line of boxes, then a grid, filled in order. Mastery is meeting new shapes and noticing they are the same four questions wearing different clothes. These are the classic next stages, in a sensible order. They are not on this site yet, but the four questions travel with you and are enough to attempt every one of them anywhere.

Stage 5

Grids with walls and prices

The Unique Paths grid again, but some cells are blocked or cost money to cross. Still two neighbors per cell; the choice flips between add and min. Classics: Unique Paths II, Minimum Path Sum.

Stage 6

Choices under a budget

Coins that must make an exact amount, items that must fit a bag. The box number stops meaning "position" and starts meaning "budget left". Classics: Coin Change, the knapsack family.

Stage 7

Two sequences side by side

Compare two words with a grid: one word down the side, one across the top, each cell scoring how well the beginnings match. Classics: Longest Common Subsequence, Edit Distance.

Stage 8

A fact you carry along

Sometimes one number per position is not enough, because you also carry a fact, like "am I currently holding the stock?". Keep one row of boxes per fact. Classics: stock problems with cooldown, Paint House.

You are getting close to mastery when

  • you can finish the sentence "dp[i] is the ___" for a brand-new problem before writing any code,
  • the story alone tells you whether to add, take the min, or take the max,
  • you fill and hand-check the first two boxes before trusting anything else,
  • and a problem you have never seen starts to feel like one you have.

You are not here to memorize templates.

A memorized template breaks the moment a problem is twisted. The four questions do not. Once naming the state and the choice feels natural, dynamic programming stops being a category of scary problems and becomes a habit you carry into new ones, including every stage on the road above.
